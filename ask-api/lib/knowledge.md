### Raw creator footage in, an editable timeline out
- slug: ai-video-editor
- status: Live | year: 2026 | client: Ad agency, in-house | Carl's role: Sole architect and builder
- headline result: ~5 min from raw footage to an editable edit. On the first real client job, a folder of raw creator takes became a Premiere timeline an editor could finish, in about five minutes, with every cut still re-trimmable.
- numbers: ~5 min first real client job, end to end; every 0.25 s a face check on camera, so a creator reading off the script gets caught before a client sees it; 5 to 10¢ model cost of the review that watches and listens to each finished ad
- summary: An eleven-step pipeline that turns a folder of unsorted takes into a Premiere timeline an editor can actually finish.
- the problem: Cutting a paid-social ad is mostly mechanical work. Someone watches every take, finds the usable one, trims the heads and tails, lays b-roll against the right line of script, then types and times the captions. It is high-volume and judgement-light, and it consumes most of the clock. The internal estimate for a single ad was roughly seven hours of pre-production spread across five or six freelancers. That was an estimate, never measured. The obvious build is a pipeline that renders a finished video. That was rejected on day one, and the whole architecture follows from rejecting it. The output must be an editable timeline, never a render, because a render cannot be fixed.
- what he built:
  - Read the storyboard from Notion: Parses the strategist's storyboard table by column header rather than by position, so an added column doesn't break the run.
  - Transcribe every take to the word: faster-whisper produces word-level timings across all takes, so the pipeline knows what was said and exactly when.
  - Match every line to its take: Each scripted line is matched deterministically against the word-level transcript of every take, so the selection is reproducible and can be audited rather than re-rolled.
  - Cut on the waveform, not the transcript: Every cut snaps to the real speech boundary in the audio and lead breath is trimmed by waveform, because transcript times are approximate and the drift accumulates.
  - Build captions from what was actually said: Karaoke captions are generated from the transcript, so a caption can never show a word the talent did not say.
  - Write a timeline, not a video: Outputs FCP7/XMEML plus a burned preview. Each source appears once in the bin at full length and is reused on the timeline, so any cut can be re-trimmed instead of re-shot.
  - Editable all the way down: October: the agency's editor asked for every cut and element to stay editable. The timeline now points at the original footage with its original audio at the right in-points, graphics arrive as stills with fades, captions as an SRT, and the file is written the way Premiere writes its own, learned from one of the editor's exports.
  - A review that watches and listens: Before an edit ships, a vision model watches the whole ad, a face check every quarter second flags eyes leaving the lens, and a speech model hears stray syllables and false starts. Every serious finding gets a second look on full-size frames before it counts, which dismisses the model's own false alarms.
  - Voice-over and text-only ads too: Producers said most of their ads are voice-over led or text on screen, so the same tool now cuts those from a recorded read and the storyboard, with every hook variant built and reviewed.
- result: Live in production since 16 July 2026 behind HTTPS and a password gate, with a job queue, a jobs page with inline preview, disk cleanup and failure reporting. The first real client job, seven scenes long, finished in about five minutes including a 1.2 GB download. The motion designer imported and relinked the handoff on a second machine in one step and signed off on the format. Sync was hardened over several rounds of real review: WAV instead of MP3 segments to kill accumulated encoder delay, frame-aligned durations, waveform lead-trim. Verified on all twelve scenes of a client ad. In October the agency's editor reviewed the rebuilt version and called it much better than the last, and it became the default way every AI edit is made. On a rejected earlier cut, the new review scored it 3 out of 10 and named the reading, the blooper and the false start.
- known limits: Colour grade is not built. The first producer-run jobs in October scored 1 to 4 out of 10 on the review; the causes were fixed and deployed, and have not been re-measured at volume. A fix verdict still needs a person: there is no automatic fix loop beyond one retry. Overlays arrive as stills, not as live Premiere text; the supported text route is being tested with the editor.
- stack: Python, ffmpeg, faster-whisper, ElevenLabs Scribe, Gemini, Claude via OpenRouter, OpenTimelineIO, FCP7 / XMEML, Docker, Dropbox API, n8n

### Publishing ads to Meta across twenty routed lanes
- slug: webhook-to-post-ads
- status: Live | year: 2026 | client: A mobile app company | Carl's role: Sole architect and builder
- headline result: 170 steps in one live publisher. An approved creative batch becomes correctly routed Meta ads across twenty lanes without anyone opening Ads Manager, and a duplicate never costs money.
- numbers: 170 nodes in a single active workflow; 20 terminal publish lanes: 2 media types x 2 platforms x 5 market routes; 2 gates a creative clears before any Meta write: status READY, then a duplicate check
- summary: A webhook-triggered publisher that pulls approved creative from Drive, uploads it to Meta, and creates ads down twenty lanes split by media type, platform and market.
- the problem: Approved creative sits in a records backend with the files themselves in Google Drive. A webhook fires when a batch is ready to go live. From there the work is mechanical and unpleasant: pull the file, upload it to Meta in the right format, find the right ad set, create the creative, create the ad, and record what happened. It is mechanical in the way that a matrix is mechanical. The same creative has to land differently depending on whether it is a video or an image, whether the campaign targets iOS or Android, and which of five market routes it belongs to. Two times two times five is twenty distinct endpoints, and the graph has twenty terminal chains to match. There is a second, quieter entry point. A Schedule Trigger runs Get Webhook then Delete Expired Webhook then Webhook Payloads: a housekeeping lane that clears expired webhook registrations so stale subscriptions do not pile up on the account. Meta will happily create the same ad twice. Nothing on the platform side stops a re-fired webhook from duplicating spend, and a freshly uploaded video is not immediately usable, because its thumbnails do not exist yet. Both problems had to be solved once, upstream of the fan-out, rather than twenty times inside it.
- what he built:
  - Gate on status, then expand the record: Get Records leads to Get Full Record and then If Status = READY. Only READY records continue. Fetch Sibling Files, Split Out and Expander then turn one approved record into the full set of files that belong with it, and Update Status = UPLOADING writes the in-progress state back before any Meta call happens, so a second webhook fire can see the record is already being worked.
  - A duplicate check before any spend: Get AdSet Meta ID (dedup) then Check Existing Ads then If Ad Is New. The false branch ends at Mark Skipped (dup) and goes no further. The true branch passes through Restore Item, a code node whose only job is to put the original creative item back on the wire after two lookup calls overwrote it with their own responses. Small node, load-bearing: without it the whole fan-out receives an API response instead of a creative.
  - Split by media, then by platform: CHECK IF VIDEO routes to one of two CHECK IF ANDROID nodes, and each of those feeds its own batch loop. Four splitInBatches loops in total, one per media-and-platform combination, each draining its own queue independently so a slow video lane does not block the image lane.
  - Video needs a wait and a thumbnail round trip: The video lanes run Download file, Upload Video Creative to Meta, Wait, Get video thumbnails, Download Thumbnail, Upload Thumbnail to Meta. The Wait node exists because Meta has not generated thumbnails at the moment upload returns. The image lanes skip all six of those nodes and go Download file, code, Upload Creative to Meta. The divergence is real work, not duplication for its own sake.
  - Five market routes per lane: Four Switch nodes, one per batch loop, each with five outputs (PT, ES, IN, TR and a MAIN default). That produces the twenty terminal chains, each with its own Create Ad Creative, its own Get Ad Set Meta ID, and its own Create Ad against the correct ad set.
  - Audit before announce: All twenty chains end identically: Create Ad, then Audit Trail writing the created ad back to the records backend, then Get Campaign Name, then a Slack success message, then back into the batch loop for the next item. The audit write happens before the Slack post, so the durable record exists whether or not anyone is reading the channel.
- result: An approved creative batch becomes a correctly routed set of Meta ads without a human opening Ads Manager. Every created ad leaves an audit row in the records backend and a Slack message naming the campaign it landed in. A repeated webhook fire costs one dedup lookup and a Mark Skipped write, not a duplicate ad.
- known limits: The twenty lanes are copies of each other. Adding a market or changing an ad payload is the same edit twenty times, and that duplication is most of why the graph is 170 nodes. Wait is a fixed delay, not a poll. If Meta has not finished processing a video when it elapses, the thumbnail fetch fails and that lane stops with no recovery. Slack is wired only to success. There is no failure message, no error branch on any of the twenty Create Ad calls, and no retry lane anywhere in the graph. The duplicate check reads and decides across two separate HTTP calls with no lock. Two webhook fires close together can both pass it.
- stack: n8n, Meta Marketing API, Google Drive, Slack, Records backend over HTTP

### A Meta reporting warehouse from eleven workflows
- slug: meta-reporting-warehouse
- status: Live | year: 2026 | client: A mobile app company | Carl's role: Sole architect and builder
- headline result: 11 workflows rebuilding reports nightly. Meta performance lands in Supabase and Airtable every night, across four lookback windows, so reporting reads a warehouse instead of the ad platform.
- numbers: 11 workflows chained into one reporting pipeline, 135 nodes in total; 8 executeWorkflow handoffs, each gated by a Limit node; 4 lookback windows written per entity: 7, 14, 28 and 30 days
- summary: Eleven small n8n workflows chained by executeWorkflow calls: purge stale rows, recreate campaign, ad set and ad records, then update each against four lookback windows.
- the problem: Reporting needed Meta campaign, ad set and ad numbers as rows in a queryable database rather than as API calls made on demand. The obvious shape for that is one large workflow. This is not that. The graph shows eleven separate workflows between eight and twenty-two nodes each. Nine of them target Supabase and are linked head to tail: every one begins with an executeWorkflowTrigger and ends with a Limit node followed by an executeWorkflow call to the next. Two more, Campaigns & Adset Sync and Performance DB Sync, run on their own schedules against Airtable. The workflows carry the author's own annotations. Sticky notes on the Supabase workflows read CAMPAIGN - PRESET, AD SET - 14D PRESET, AD SET - 28D PRESET, AD SET - 30D PRESET: the naming of a system meant to be recognised at a glance six months later. n8n runs on items, and that fights you the moment you chain workflows. If a stage finishes with 400 rows on the wire, a naive executeWorkflow call fires the next stage 400 times. Every single handoff in this chain is a Limit node immediately followed by the call, which collapses the stream to one item so the next stage runs exactly once. Eight handoffs, eight Limits.
- what he built:
  - One scheduled head, eight handoffs: Meta Insights - Deletion - Supabase (the graph pictured here) is the only workflow in the Supabase chain with a Schedule Trigger. It clears ads, ad sets and campaigns through three batched delete loops plus a data-table row clear, then hands off to the campaign create workflow. From there the line runs campaign, ad set, ad, into the update chain, then campaign, ad set 14D, 28D, 30D, ads. Nothing in the middle has its own trigger.
  - Rebuild rather than reconcile: The chain deletes and recreates instead of diffing. For tables that are only ever read by reporting, that removes an entire class of drift bugs: there is no state to get subtly wrong over months. The cost is paid honestly and is listed below.
  - Four lookback windows as four passes: Creation writes the 7-day numbers. The update chain then walks 14, 28 and 30 day windows as three separate workflows of near-identical shape: fetch ad sets for the campaign, fetch metrics, fetch a country breakdown, fetch a device breakdown, merge, flatten in code, update the row.
  - Three API surfaces merged per ad set: Each ad-set workflow fires four parallel HTTP calls straight off its trigger (the ad set list plus metrics, country breakdown and device breakdown) into one Merge node, then a single code node flattens them into one row. Meta will not return breakdowns alongside plain metrics in a single call, so the merge is doing the join the API refuses to.
  - Idempotency where rows get created: The create-side workflows check before they write: If row exists guards the ad-set insert, and Insert row records the campaign key in a data table. Deletions go through splitInBatches loops rather than one bulk call, so a large purge is paced rather than fired at the API in one shot.
  - A second warehouse in Airtable, built the opposite way: Campaigns & Adset Sync runs on its own schedule and reconciles instead of rebuilding: fetch campaigns from Meta, fetch existing rows from Airtable, diff them in Find New Campaigns, then branch New or Existing Campaign? into a create or an update. It repeats the pattern for ad sets with one extra call, Fetch All Campaigns from Airtable (for linking), whose only purpose is to resolve parent record IDs so each ad set row can point at its campaign.
  - Performance rows as a straight upsert: Performance DB Sync fires daily at 2AM, fans a single insights call into four parallel lookups (upload queue, campaigns, ad sets, existing performance rows), merges all four, and lets Process Insights decide per record whether New or Existing Record? routes to Create Performance Record or Update Performance Record.
- result: Reporting queries Supabase and Airtable instead of the Meta API. Each entity level is its own workflow with its own trigger, so a broken ad-set pass can be re-run on its own without touching the campaign pass above it. Ad-set rows carry country and device breakdowns alongside plain metrics, joined at write time rather than at read time.
- known limits: The chain is linear and unguarded. If a workflow in the middle fails, everything downstream simply never runs, and no node in the graph reports that. Delete-then-recreate leaves a window where campaign, ad set and ad rows are gone. Anything querying during the rebuild sees an incomplete warehouse. The three ad-set update workflows are near-identical copies differing only in the window. A change to the row shape is the same change in three places. Two warehouses, Supabase and Airtable, are populated by independent pipelines and never reconciled against each other.
- stack: n8n, Meta Marketing API, Supabase, Airtable REST API, n8n Data Tables

### Creator sourcing from scrape to payment
- slug: creator-sourcing-to-payment-pipeline
- status: Live | year: 2025 to present | client: Ad agency | Carl's role: Sole architect and builder
- headline result: 7 stages, from scrape to paid. Building a creator roster became a pipeline run instead of a week of DMs, with one record following each creator from first scrape to payment.
- numbers: 7 stages from first scrape to CRM record; 1 creator record every step reads and writes; 3 systems kept in step: database, payments, CRM
- summary: An agent-run pipeline that finds UGC creators, enriches and onboards them, sends the proposal, handles payment, and files the finished record into the CRM.
- the problem: An ad agency running UGC at volume needs a fresh roster of creators for every brand it works with. Sourcing was a manual chain: search the platform, check whether the creator fits, find contact details, send a DM, chase, agree terms, send a proposal, invoice, pay, then re-key the whole thing into the CRM. Each link in that chain lived in a different tool, and the handoffs were where things died. A creator would go quiet between the proposal and the contract and nobody could say which step they were stuck on, because no single system held the whole story. The build was to make the chain one pipeline with one record, run by agents, with the CRM at the end of it rather than in the middle. Contracts and money sit inside this flow: Every step had to be idempotent and auditable, because a retry that sends a second proposal is embarrassing and a retry that sends a second payment is worse.
- what he built:
  - Scrape a shortlist, not a list: The sourcing pass runs against criteria set per brand, so what comes back is a shortlist to review rather than a scrape dump somebody has to filter by hand.
  - Enrich before a human looks: Contact details, platform stats and fit signals are attached to the row before it reaches a person, so the first human decision is a judgement call and not a research task.
  - One record, one state machine: A Supabase row per creator holds the state: sourced, contacted, onboarded, proposed, signed, paid. Every downstream step reads and writes that row, which is what makes 'where is this creator stuck' a question with an answer.
  - Onboarding and proposal from the record: Onboarding collects what the brief needs, and the proposal is generated from the creator record and the brand's terms rather than assembled by hand per creator.
  - Payment keyed to the record: The payment step is tied to the creator's record and its state, so a retried run settles the same obligation instead of creating a new one.
  - CRM last, as a reader: HubSpot receives a complete record at the end. It reports on the pipeline; it does not coordinate it. That distinction is what stops the CRM filling with half-finished contacts.
- result: Building a creator roster for a brand is a pipeline run rather than a week of DMs and spreadsheet upkeep. One record follows a creator from first scrape to paid, so the question of who is stuck where has a single place to look. Contract and payment steps are safe to retry, which means a failed run can be re-run rather than untangled by hand. The CRM holds finished records instead of half-populated ones, which makes reporting on it worth doing.
- known limits: Scraped fit signals are proxies. A creator who looks right on the data can still deliver footage that the QA pass rejects, and that judgement is still a human's. The pipeline was shaped around one agency's proposal terms and payment setup. A different agency's terms would mean rewriting those steps, not configuring them. No baseline was captured before the pipeline existed, so there is no honest comparison against manual sourcing on cost or conversion. Platform scraping is inherently fragile. A layout or API change breaks the front of the funnel, and it needs watching rather than trusting.
- stack: Claude Code agents, Supabase (Postgres), HubSpot, Payment provider API, Scraping + enrichment APIs, n8n

### Ad scripts that pass a scorecard before a strategist reads them
- slug: concepts-and-scripts-engine
- status: Live | year: 2026 | client: Ad agency, in-house | Carl's role: Sole builder
- headline result: 10 point scorecard on every script. Every ad script is graded on direct-response craft and swept for compliance before a strategist reads it, then filed as a doc in the team's own layout.
- numbers: 10 points on the direct-response scorecard every script is graded against; the four that clients complained about most must reach 8; 4 words/s the spoken-word budget, checked in code and calibrated on scripts the team had already got approved
- summary: A writing pipeline for paid-social ads that runs the strategists' own skill word for word, then makes every draft earn its way past a ten-point direct-response scorecard and a compliance sweep before anyone has to fix it.
- the problem: AI-written ad scripts got the who and the what right and fell apart on the how and the proof. Batch after batch drifted into the same shape: a problem, the product finds the right people, real engagement, an offer in the last line. Strategists rewrote them by hand, and by their own estimate each fix took fifteen to twenty minutes. The scripts also ran long. Generated batches came back far wordier than the batches the same team had written and the client had approved, which only shows when somebody tries to read one aloud in thirty seconds. The first fix that failed was adding more judges. A chain of reviewers and rewriters on top of the writer turned good drafts into flat ones, and the concept stage went back to running the skill as written, with the reviewers reduced to flags. The strategists' skill is the source of truth. Code only checks what code can count.
- what he built:
  - A contract per concept before a word is written: Each concept gets one line stating what the ad tests, for whom, and the measurable proof that would close the argument. The draft is held to that line from hook to call to action, so the proof has to match the promise instead of defaulting to likes and comments.
  - The team's own skill, installed word for word: The writer runs the strategists' skill exactly as they wrote it, including its pre-write analysis of persona, objections and awareness level. An earlier version paraphrased the reference files for the pipeline and lost the craft rules, so now nothing is rewritten.
  - Who can be on camera, per client: A production brief for each client, extracted from what the agency actually shot for them, states who may appear, how many people, and where. The concept selector scores cast fit and refuses a winner below four, and a lint flags a second actor in a one-creator brief.
  - A ten-point scorecard with a high bar on four: A separate reviewer grades every script on the scorecard. Proof, differentiation, product introduction and offer placement must reach 8; anything under goes back to the writer with the failing lines quoted.
  - A compliance sweep with no ship-anyway option: Last, a QA pass checks banned words, claims, disclaimers, grammar and whether each script still makes sense end to end. A script that fails is rewritten and re-scored; one that cannot pass is killed and flagged rather than delivered.
  - Filed where the team works: Finished batches become a Google Doc in the house layout, filed in the client's batch folder, so a strategist opens a document instead of a tool.
- result: Script batches are delivered on request as Google Docs in the team's format, with a summary of each script's contract, proof and offer placement. Length is now held to the team's own approved range by a word budget checked in code, rather than by asking the model to be brief.
- known limits: Strategists ask for runs rather than running it themselves, so it works as a service, not yet as a self-serve tool. Whether the model may write plausible illustrative numbers or must leave a placeholder is a policy question that is still open. A full eight-script run costs several dollars in model spend, which matters at batch volume.
- stack: Node, Claude, Google Docs API, Supabase, Docker

### Your photos in, an original song written about them out
- slug: songreels
- status: Live · private beta | year: 2026 | client: Consumer product (SongReels) | Carl's role: Sole builder; product direction shared with the founder
- headline result: $9.99 per gift, launched end to end. A consumer product taken from idea to a live beta: the song is written about your own photos, and the reel arrives as a gift page that opens with no signup and no app.
- numbers: $9.99 the published price of one gift, one-time (checkout built, switched off in beta); 3 questions written from your own clips after they have been watched, instead of from a template
- summary: A consumer gift product where the song is written about the photos you upload, and the cut is made against the finished song rather than planned before it.
- the problem: A personal gift video normally means one of two things. Either somebody spends an evening in an editor cutting a camera roll to a track that was written about somebody else's life, or they use a template that lays the same photos over the same stock music. Both end up as a slideshow, and both are obvious about it. The other version is a song that is actually about the photos: the line about their kitchen playing while the kitchen is on screen. That is the version people ask for, and it puts the difficulty in a new place: the song and the pictures now have to agree with each other. It also matters that the person paying is not the person watching. The buyer wants something honest to send; the recipient gets a link, usually on a phone, from someone they love, and will not install anything to see it. That shapes the whole output: the product is a page that plays, not a file to email. The obvious build matches lyric words against clip descriptions at render time and drops each clip where its words are sung. That was built. Fuzzy matching missed the near misses ("yellow wall" against "yellow building"), clips scattered unevenly across the timeline, and the finished reel carried three to five second black gaps. The lyrics are written in the order the clips will play, so sync is a property of the writing rather than something the renderer has to go and find.
- what he built:
  - Watch the clips first, then ask about what is in them: On leaving the upload step the browser pulls one frame from up to eight clips, evenly sampled across the upload, and posts those small JPEGs to a vision service along with the occasion the sender already picked. Back comes a one-line read of what the clips are about and exactly three questions grounded in what it saw, with a "that's not quite it" editor if the read is wrong. If the vision pass fails for any reason (no key, a model error, a timeout, unparseable output), generic occasion questions take over rather than the flow stopping.
  - Let the footage set the length of the song: Gemini watches every full clip and returns the window worth showing. The model path runs through Parse Decision and rejoins that same Finalize node, so both paths converge on one decision format before anything acts.
  - Log before acting, not after: Finalize feeds Log Decision (KEEP+DELETE), a Google Sheets append, and only then DELETE or KEEP?. Every decision is written down before the delete call fires, including the ones the model never saw. If the delete succeeds you still hold the record of why it was made.
  - A failed delete is deliberately not marked processed: Delete Comment has two outputs. Success goes to Mark Processed. The error output goes to Delete Failed (retry next run), a no-op that writes nothing, so the comment is absent from the processed table and the next scheduled run picks it up again. KEEP decisions go straight to Mark Processed. That asymmetry is the whole retry mechanism, and it costs one node.
- result: Every comment that enters the pipeline ends in one of three states: marked processed with a KEEP, marked processed after a successful delete, or left unmarked so the next run retries it. The sheet holds the full decision log, including the comments the pre-screen resolved without a model call. Re-running the workflow is harmless. Nothing depends on the schedule having fired exactly once.
- known limits: The 500 cap is a fixed number in a Limit node. If a run's backlog is larger, the remainder silently waits for the next run, and nothing in the graph reports the overflow. The 14-day recency filter is hardcoded. Older spam is permanently out of scope rather than queued for a slower pass. There is no alert path anywhere. Delete Failed is a no-op named after its intent; if a comment fails to delete on every run, nothing tells a human. One model provider (OpenRouter) with no fallback node. If it errors, the ambiguous branch stops while the pre-screened branches keep flowing, so the run half-works, which is harder to notice than a clean failure.
- stack: n8n, Meta Graph API, OpenRouter (LLM), n8n Data Tables, Google Sheets

### Footage renamer for creator uploads
- slug: footage-renamer
- status: Live | year: 2026 | client: Ad agency | Carl's role: Sole architect and builder
- headline result: 0 original files ever overwritten. Raw creator clips become a correctly named, storyboard-matched folder, and QA sees missing shots at upload time instead of in the middle of an edit.
- numbers: 58 unit checks passing on the rename logic; 14 scenes parsed live from a real storyboard; 0.6 confidence floor below which a clip is flagged
- summary: A Notion button turns a creator's mis-named Dropbox uploads into a clean, storyboard-matched footage folder, plus a report of every shot that is still missing.
- the problem: When a creator finishes a shoot they upload a folder of clips named DJI_0001.MOV, clip_take_a.mov, and worse. The agency's QA team then watched every clip, worked out which storyboard scene it belonged to, and renamed it by hand so the design and edit stages could find it. The storyboard is the ground truth, and it already exists, as a table in the job's Notion page, in the format the strategists already write. Nothing was wrong with the storyboard; the work was matching footage to it. Worse, missing shots were only discovered later, mid-edit, when a scene had no footage. That is the most expensive moment to find out. One line was not negotiable: A vision model may suggest which scene a clip belongs to, but the filename it produces must be generated by code, from the storyboard's own words.
- what he built:
  - Trigger where the work already lives: A Notion button on the job row sets Status to Queued. n8n polls every two minutes, and the first thing it does is set Status to Processing, which claims the job so two ticks can never pick up the same row.
  - Parse the storyboard by header, not by column order: The storyboard table is read by header name, so an added column or a reordered one cannot break it. Multi-shot cells split on ' + ' and commas. Talking-head versus b-roll is inferred from whether the Footage Name cell is filled, so strategists author nothing extra.
  - Shrink, then look: Inline payloads to the vision model are size-capped, so ffmpeg reduces each clip to a low-res proxy or a handful of sampled frames first. One model call per clip, matched against the closed set of that storyboard's scenes, returning a scene, a shot slug and a confidence.
  - Deterministic rename in one tested module: Slugging, multi-shot splitting, take grouping (_take1, _take2), version suffixes (_v2), the output folder layout and the missing-shot diff all live in one Node module with 58 passing unit checks. The same module is pasted into the n8n code node and runs headless on the server, so there is one source of truth for naming.
  - Never destroy the original: Output is a new Dropbox folder (aroll/, broll/ and a _report.md) built by copying from the creator's upload, never moving it. Every take is kept; the tool organises, it does not choose the keeper.
  - A confidence floor, then a human: Below 0.6 confidence a clip keeps its original name and is listed in the report. The job row goes to 'Needs review' rather than 'Done', and QA gets a share link plus the report as a comment on the row.
- result: The storyboard parser was validated live against a real job page: 14 scenes, 17 b-roll shots, multi-shot cells split correctly. Stage one runs inside the agency's own n8n instance, triggered from the Notion queue the strategists already use. QA receives a report naming the storyboard shots that no clip matched, at upload time rather than in the middle of an edit. Naming is deterministic, so the downstream assembly editor, which matches b-roll by filename, locks on without a human re-checking.
- known limits: The clip-matching stage has been validated on fixtures and one real storyboard, not across a full slate of creator uploads. Running a real creator's footage end to end is the next step, not a finished one. Talking-head matching needs the spoken audio and is specified but unproven. The tested path is b-roll, matched on what is visible on screen. Runtime scales with footage size, because clips are downloaded and re-uploaded rather than moved in place. Large jobs can hit the n8n execution timeout and need the limit raised. The 0.6 confidence floor is a starting guess, chosen to fail toward human review. It has not been tuned against enough real jobs to defend the number.
- stack: n8n, Notion API, Dropbox API (OAuth2), Gemini Flash via OpenRouter, ffmpeg, Node.js

### AI SDR agent on a two-way CRM bridge
- slug: ai-sdr-and-crm-bridge
- status: Live | year: 2024 to present | client: Marketing consultancy | Carl's role: Sole architect and builder
- headline result: 2-way CRM sync, one record per contact. A reply gets a relevant answer the hour it arrives, and the deal stage moves in the same run instead of at the next manual review.
- numbers: 2-way CRM sync with origin-tagged writes; 4 agent steps: detect, classify, follow up, book; 3 go-to-market platforms on one contact record
- summary: An email agent that spots a real reply, classifies intent, follows up and books the call, on a two-way bridge that keeps two CRMs in step.
- the problem: A marketing consultancy ran outbound for itself and for its clients. Replies arrived at every hour and got answered whenever somebody next opened the inbox, which is usually the wrong hour for a warm lead. Underneath that, the record was split. The funnel builder held the signup, GoHighLevel held the conversation, and HubSpot held the pipeline. The same person existed in more than one place with different facts attached, so any automation built on top of one system was building on a partial truth. So the work ran in two halves: make one contact mean one record, then let an agent handle the first move on a reply. Both platforms believed they owned the contact: The bridge had to sync in both directions without looping, so a write caused by a sync must never trigger another sync back.
- what he built:
  - Fix the record before automating the conversation: HubSpot first: lifecycle stages, pipelines, the properties automations key off, and dashboards that read from those properties. An agent writing into an inconsistent CRM just produces inconsistency faster.
  - A bridge, not a nightly export: Bidirectional sync between the funnel platform and GoHighLevel, built on an explicit field map with one owning system per field and origin-tagged writes, so a change that arrived from a sync is recognised as such and not echoed back.
  - Reply detection that is not a keyword match: Before anything is classified, the agent separates a human reply from auto-responders, bounces, out-of-office notices and threading artifacts. Most of the reliability of an SDR agent is spent here, not on the writing.
  - Classify, then branch: Intent classes drive the action: interested goes to booking, not-now goes to a nurture sequence, an objection gets a drafted response, unsubscribe suppresses the contact everywhere at once rather than in the one platform where it was said.
  - Booking is a write: When the lead is ready, the agent puts the meeting on the calendar and moves the deal stage in the same run, so the pipeline reflects reality without anyone re-keying it afterwards.
  - Reporting off the data, not the tool: Supabase-backed dashboards read the underlying data, so what the team reports on does not depend on any one CRM's native views, and survives a platform change.
- result: A reply gets a relevant answer at the hour it arrives, and the CRM stage moves at the same moment rather than at the next manual review. One contact means one record across both platforms; a change on either side is visible on the other without an overnight export. Unsubscribes and suppressions apply everywhere, which is a compliance property as much as a convenience one. The same pattern is reused across client accounts instead of each one being hand-built from scratch.
- known limits: Reply classification was never audited against a labelled set, so no accuracy figure is claimed. It handles the common intents; the unusual ones are meant to reach a human. The share of replies that gets routed to a person has not been tracked, so the honest description is 'agent-first with human fallback', not 'autonomous'. Bidirectional sync is only as safe as the field-ownership map. Add a field on one side without deciding who owns it and the two systems can overwrite each other. None of this is a public artifact. It lives inside client accounts, so it can be demonstrated live but not linked to.
- stack: HubSpot, GoHighLevel, Systeme.io, n8n, Supabase, LLM intent classification, Calendar booking API
